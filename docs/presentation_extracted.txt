Abstract
Laminar-to-turbulent boundary-layer transition influences skin-friction drag, heat transfer,
and flow separation, and therefore affects the performance of a wide range of engineering
systems, including aircraft wings, turbomachinery blades, marine vehicles, and internal flow
passages. The prediction and characterization of transition are traditionally pursued through
three established approaches: empirical correlations, transition-sensitive computational fluid
dynamics (CFD), and wind-tunnel experimentation. Although each approach possesses dis-
tinct strengths and limitations, their combined application typically requires substantial manual
effort to transfer information between prediction, simulation, experimental planning and exe-
cution, data acquisition, post-processing, and comparative assessment. This dependence on
manual coordination makes transition investigations time-consuming and complicates the inte-
grated interpretation of transition behaviour.
The present work addresses this limitation through the development of an agentic artificial-
intelligence framework for automating boundary-layer transition research workflows. A pipeline
comprising six specialised agents and a central orchestration layer is developed to coordinate
agent activities, manage information exchange between agents, and provide robust recovery
mechanisms at critical stages of the workflow. The framework accepts a user-defined transition
problem, decomposes it into specialised tasks, manages information exchange between agents,
and maintains traceability between literature sources, numerical calculations, experimental
measurements, and reported conclusions.
The empirical-prediction branch evaluates established transition correlations through a
retrieval-augmented reasoning architecture grounded in the scientific literature.
The CFD
branch employs transition-sensitive Reynolds–Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) simulations us-
ing established transition models implemented in OpenFOAM. The experimental branch sup-
ports human-in-the-loop wind-tunnel experimentation through automated traverse planning,
acquisition-script generation, intermittency-based transition detection, and boundary-layer
profile analysis. Following acquisition, the hot-wire voltage signals are converted to velocity
signals via King’s-law calibration to obtain boundary-layer profiles and transition character-
istics. Integral boundary-layer quantities — boundary-layer thickness, displacement thickness,
momentum thickness, shape factor, and momentum-thickness Reynolds number — are evaluated
alongside single-point statistics, power spectral densities, and a conditional acceleration-
variance diagnostic ⟨(𝜕𝑢/𝜕𝑡)2⟩to characterise transitional behaviour.
iii

iv
The outputs of the individual agents are subsequently assembled within a dedicated cross-
validation stage, where empirical predictions, CFD estimates, and experimental observations
are compared and reconciled. The resulting workflow enables the generation of a consolidated
technical report that preserves traceability between published literature, numerical simulations,
experimental measurements, and the final conclusions.
The developed framework is demonstrated on a free-stream-turbulence-induced bypass-
transition case (Tu = 2.7 %) over a flat plate under zero-pressure-gradient conditions in the
LT1 wind tunnel of the Low-Speed Aerodynamics Laboratory, Indian Institute of Science. The
workflow is used to coordinate empirical prediction, transition-sensitive CFD analysis, human-
in-the-loop experimentation, data acquisition, data processing, and report generation within
a single integrated environment. Although developed and demonstrated for boundary-layer
transition studies, the underlying agentic architecture is sufficiently general to support other
research workflows requiring the coordinated application of literature-driven reasoning, nu-
merical simulation, experimentation, and data analysis.
Key words: Boundary-layer transition, bypass transition, agentic AI, retrieval-augmented
generation, OpenFOAM, hot-wire anemometry, intermittency, transition-sensitive CFD, work-
flow automation, boundary-layer characterization.

Chapter 1
Introduction
This chapter introduces boundary-layer transition and reviews the three principal routes
through whichitisconventionallyanalyzed: empiricalcorrelations, transition-sensitiveReynolds–
Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models, and hot-wire experimental detection. Recent devel-
opments in retrieval-augmented reasoning and agentic scientific workflows are also surveyed,
since they provide the foundation for the framework developed in the present work. The chapter
then identifies the research gaps addressed by the thesis, states the objectives of the study, and
outlines the structure of the report.
1.1
Background
Laminar-to-turbulent transition of a boundary layer remains one of the fundamental problems
of fluid mechanics. The state of the boundary layer influences the skin-friction drag, heat-
transfer rate, and separation behavior of wall-bounded flows (Schlichting and Gersten, 2017)
[46], and therefore affects the performance of a wide range of engineering systems including
aircraft wings, turbine blades, compressors, marine vehicles, and internal flow passages (Mayle,
1991) [35]. Accurate knowledge of the transition location is consequently important both for
aerodynamic design and for the interpretation of experimental measurements.
The prediction of transition is complicated by the variety of mechanisms through which
it can occur. Under low-disturbance conditions, transition proceeds through the amplification
and breakdown of Tollmien–Schlichting waves, first demonstrated by Schubauer and Skramstad
(1947) [47]. The classical six-stage progression of natural transition on a flat plate is shown in
Figure 1.1.

In practical engineering flows, however, the freestream turbulence level is often sufficiently
high for transition to occur through the bypass route, in which streamwise elongated streaks
(Klebanoff modes) are amplified within the laminar boundary layer through receptivity to
freestream vortical disturbances, and subsequently break down into turbulent spots (Matsubara
and Alfredsson, 2001) [34]. The bypass route is visualized through direct numerical simu-
lation in Figure 1.2. The location and extent of transition therefore depend not only on the
Reynolds number but also on the turbulence environment, pressure-gradient distribution, sur-
face condition, and upstream flow history (Abu-Ghannam and Shaw, 1980) [1], (Mayle, 1991)
[35].

Three complementary approaches are commonly employed to evaluate transition. Empirical
correlations estimate the onset of transition from a limited set of flow parameters and remain
attractive because of their simplicity and low computational cost. Transition-sensitive Reynolds-
averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models provide a higher-fidelity alternative by resolving the
development of the boundary layer and its interaction with local turbulence and pressure-
gradient effects. Experimental techniques, particularly hot-wire anemometry, provide direct
measurements of the transition process and continue to serve as the primary reference against
which predictive approaches are assessed (Schlichting and Gersten, 2017) [46].
A transition correlation may be used to obtain a preliminary estimate of onset, a CFD simu-
lation may subsequently be configured to examine the flow in greater detail, and an experiment
may finally be performed to assess the accuracy of the predictions. The transfer of information
between these stages is often manual, making the overall workflow labor-intensive and difficult
to reproduce consistently (Slotnick et al., 2014) [49].
The present work considers the integration of these traditionally separate activities within a
single workflow. To this end, a multi-agent framework is developed in which specialized agents
perform theoretical prediction, CFD analysis, experimental planning, data processing, and cross-
validation, while a central orchestration layer manages the exchange of information between
them.
The framework is designed to support the transition-analysis process by organizing
the flow of information between theoretical, numerical, and experimental branches, thereby

enabling predictions, measurements, and validation results to be incorporated within a common
workflow.
The remainder of this chapter reviews the physical mechanisms of transition, the principal
approaches used for its prediction and measurement, and the recent developments in agentic
scientific workflows that provide the foundation for the framework developed in this thesis. The
chapter then identifies the research gaps addressed by the present work, states the objectives of
the study, and outlines the structure of the thesis.
1.2
Literature Review
1.2.1
Boundary-Layer Transition
The picture of transition outlined in §1.1 is the product of more than seven decades of careful
experimentation and theoretical analysis. The existence of the Tollmien–Schlichting wave was
first demonstrated by Schubauer and Skramstad (1947) [47] in low-disturbance wind-tunnel
measurements that resolved the linear-amplification phase of natural transition for the first
time. Subsequent work shifted attention to the late stages of the transition process. Klebanoff
(1954) [26] showed that the streamwise distribution of intermittency exhibits a universal form
when expressed in suitable non-dimensional coordinates, building on the intermittency factor
introduced earlier by Emmons (1951) [11]. Narasimha (1957) [38] and Narasimha (1985) [39]
condensed the universal distribution into the simple exponential expression that is now used
almost without exception in transition analysis; the universal collapse is reproduced as Figure
1.3.
The comprehensive review of Mayle (1991) [35] drew together the bypass-transition
literature in a turbomachinery context, and a modern overview of both natural and bypass routes
is given by Saric, Reed and White (2003) [44].

A practical consequence of this body of work is that the transition region can be described
by four scalar quantities — onset, completion, length, and the intermittency distribution that
connects them — and that these quantities provide the common basis on which empirical corre-
lations, numerical models, and experimental measurements may be compared. The remainder
of this review surveys each of these three predictive routes in turn.
1.2.2
Empirical Correlations
The earliest predictive approach, and the one that retains the widest practical adoption, is
the empirical correlation of transition onset against a small number of free-stream parameters.
Abu-Ghannam and Shaw (1980) [1] constructed such a correlation for zero-pressure-gradient
flows from an extensive collection of flat-plate experiments; their fit relates the momentum-
thickness Reynolds number at transition onset to the local free-stream turbulence intensity and
continues to serve as a standard reference. The correlation of Mayle (1991) [35], constructed
primarily from turbomachinery data, addresses the bypass-dominated regime and is most reliable
in the moderate-to-high turbulence-intensity range characteristic of that application; below
approximately one per cent free-stream turbulence intensity the bypass mechanism is no longer
dominant and the correlation is generally regarded as inapplicable.
A more recent class of correlations recognizes that the free-stream turbulence length scale,
in addition to its intensity, exerts a measurable influence on the location and width of the
transition region. Fransson and Shahinfar (2020) [16] developed both a turbulence-intensity-
only fit and a length-scale-dependent variant for the mid-transition Reynolds number; their work
is significant for the present purpose because it adopts a different convention from the earlier

Chapter 1. Introduction
6
correlations, reporting the mid-transition state rather than the onset state. The intermittency
distribution of Narasimha (1985) [39] provides the bridge between these conventions, allowing
onset, mid-transition, and near-completion locations to be compared on a common basis. The
full mathematical forms of the correlations are reproduced in Appendix A. Despite their utility,
empirical correlations cannot resolve the boundary-layer development itself, and numerical
simulation has therefore long been regarded as the natural next step.
1.2.3
Transition Modeling in RANS
Direct and large-eddy simulations of canonical transitional boundary layers have become
increasingly accessible in recent years, but the computational cost of fully resolved unsteady
simulation remains prohibitive at industrial Reynolds numbers. Reynolds-averaged Navier–
Stokes modeling therefore continues to play the central role in transition prediction, and a
number of dedicated transition-sensitive closures have been developed.
Walters and Cokljat (2008) [53] introduced a three-equation eddy-viscosity model, com-
monly identified as the 𝑘–𝑘𝐿–𝜔model, which augments a baseline two-equation closure with
an additional transport equation for a laminar kinetic energy. The transfer of laminar kinetic
energy into turbulent kinetic energy provides the mechanism by which bypass transition is
represented within a Reynolds-averaged framework.
Langtry and Menter (2009) [30] sub-
sequently formulated a correlation-based transition model in which two additional transport
equations describe an intermittency variable and a transitional momentum-thickness Reynolds
number; the model is coupled to the 𝑘–𝜔shear-stress-transport turbulence closure of Menter
(1994) [36] and is widely referred to as the 𝛾–f
𝑅𝑒𝜃𝑡model. Both formulations have been ex-
tensively validated against the ERCOFTAC T3A and T3B flat-plate cases (Savill, 1993) [45],
which now serve as the standard benchmark for transitional Reynolds-averaged predictions in
zero-pressure-gradient flows. These models offer the higher fidelity that empirical correlations
cannot provide, but their predictions still depend on free-stream turbulence specifications and
modeling assumptions that ultimately require experimental confirmation.
1.2.4
Experimental Investigation
Experimental data therefore remain the authoritative reference against which both empirical
and numerical predictions are assessed. For low-speed flat-plate transition studies, hot-wire
anemometry has long been the dominant measurement technique, on account of its high temporal
resolution and its favorable frequency response. The voltage–velocity relationship of the heated
sensor is most commonly described by a King’s-law calibration (Bruun, 1995) [5], in which
the calibration constants are obtained from a sweep performed against a reference velocity such
as a Pitot-static probe. Polynomial calibrations have been proposed as alternatives, but the

Chapter 1. Introduction
7
physics-constrained King’s-law form is more robust outside the calibrated range and remains
the recommended choice for traverses that extend to near-wall stations.
The extraction of turbulent intermittency from a hot-wire trace was studied extensively in
the 1970s and 1980s. The detector of Hedley and Keffer (1974) [19] employed a high-pass-
filtered detector function with a manually chosen threshold, but the dependence of the resulting
intermittency on that threshold motivated subsequent refinements. Fransson, Matsubara and
Alfredsson (2005) [15] introduced a procedure in which the threshold is swept over a range
of values and the true intermittency is extrapolated from the well-behaved exponential decay
region of the intermittency–threshold curve, removing the need for a single arbitrary choice.
This procedure has since become the standard intermittency-detection scheme in flat-plate
boundary-layer studies and is reproduced in detail in Appendix A.
The measured intermittency distribution is conventionally interpreted within the Narasimha
(1985) [39] framework to extract onset, mid-transition, and near-completion locations, together
with the characteristic transition length. Wall-normal traverses additionally provide the velocity
profiles required to evaluate the integral boundary-layer quantities — displacement thickness,
momentum thickness, shape factor, and momentum-thickness Reynolds number — that con-
stitute the conventional description of boundary-layer development (Schlichting and Gersten,
2017) [46]. Higher-order statistics such as skewness and flatness are also routinely evaluated at
each station; the flatness factor in particular provides an independent indicator of intermittent
turbulent activity, and is used in the present work as a complementary transition marker.
1.2.5
Agentic Scientific Workflows
The empirical, numerical, and experimental routes surveyed above have developed largely as
parallel streams of inquiry. Cross-validation between them is widely acknowledged as desirable
but is, in practice, performed manually for individual flow cases, with information passing
between the three streams through written reports and human judgment. Recent advances in
large language models have raised the possibility of automating parts of this exchange.
The general paradigm of interleaving language-model reasoning with explicit tool invo-
cations was introduced by Yao et al. (2023) [56] under the name ReAct, and now underlies
a substantial fraction of modern agent architectures.
Lewis et al.
(2020) [31] formalized
retrieval-augmented generation, in which an external corpus of curated documents is queried
during reasoning so that the model’s output can be grounded in verifiable sources rather than in
the parametric memory of the model alone. Multi-agent orchestration frameworks subsequently
extended these ideas to workflows in which several specialized agents are coordinated through a
deterministic graph-based control layer; the orchestrator developed in the present work is built
on top of one such framework.
The application of these ideas to scientific problems is an active research area. Bran et al.

Chapter 1. Introduction
8
(2024) [4] demonstrated an autonomous agent that couples a language model to a library of
chemistry tools to design synthesis routes; comparable architectures have since been described in
molecular biology, materials science, and observational astronomy. To the author’s knowledge,
however, no published work has so far applied a multi-agent architecture to the cross-validation
of boundary-layer transition predictions across theoretical, numerical, and experimental routes.
This is the gap that the present thesis is intended to address.
A recurring concern in the deployment of language-model agents within a scientific workflow
is the prevention of unsupported or fabricated content, commonly described in the literature
as hallucination. The conventional response combines retrieval-augmented generation with
explicit source tracking of every reported quantity, deterministic verification of any numerical
value, and a dedicated critique stage in which the produced output is examined for completeness
and correctness. These safeguards form a recurring theme in the design of the framework
presented here and are described in detail in Chapter 3.
1.2.6
State-of-the-Art Multi-Agent Scientific AI Frameworks
The architecture of decomposing a scientific workflow into specialized tool-using agents —
each owning a narrowly scoped task and communicating through structured artifact passing —
has matured rapidly since 2023 across several scientific fields. In chemistry, Bran et al. (2024)
[4] introduced ChemCrow, an LLM augmented with eighteen expert-designed chemistry tools
that autonomously plans and executes organic syntheses, and Boiko et al. (2023) [3] developed
Coscientist, which independently designs, plans, and runs catalyzed cross-coupling reactions on
wet-lab robotics. In materials science, Kang and Kim (2023) [25] introduced ChatMOF, an LLM
agent paired with a domain toolkit that predicts and generates metal-organic frameworks from
natural-language queries. The biomedical line followed shortly after: BioPlanner (O’Donoghue
et al., 2023) [40] established a framework for evaluating LLM-generated experimental protocols
on the BioProt dataset; CRISPR-GPT (Qu et al., 2024) [41] automates gene-editing experiment
design end-to-end; AgentMD (Jin et al., 2024) [21] selects the appropriate clinical risk calculator
out of 2,164 candidates with 87.7 % accuracy on the RiskQA benchmark against 40.9 % for
standalone GPT-4; and Ferber et al. (2024) [14] demonstrated a comparable agent-based system
for oncology decision-making. The recurring architectural pattern across all of these fields is
the same — specialized tool-using agents, structured artifact passing, retrieval grounding in
curated literature, and explicit audit records for every reported result.
In fluid mechanics the equivalent line of work is younger and has progressed along three
distinct sub-lines.
The CFD-setup line automates the configuration of a single CFD case from a natural-
language description. Chen et al. (2024) [6] introduced MetaOpenFOAM, the first LLM-based
multi-agent framework to set up OpenFOAM cases, evaluated across eight benchmark scenarios.

1.2.7
Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Scientific Literature
Conventionalretrieval-augmentedgeneration(RAG)pipelines, originallydesignedforgeneral-
purpose question answering over web text, perform poorly when applied to scientific papers.
The canonical RAG pipeline is summarized in Figure 1.4: documents from a curated knowledge
base are split into chunks and embedded by a learned encoder, the user query is embedded by
the same encoder, and the top matches are retrieved from a vector store and supplied to the gen-
erator as additional context. Three structural limitations of this pipeline are well-documented
in the recent literature. First, conventional RAG is fundamentally text-only and discards the
multimodal content — figures, charts, equations, and spatial layouts — that carries the core
findings of scientific papers; the naive workaround of converting such content into captions
causes severe information loss (Han et al., 2025) [18]. Second, fixed-size chunking fragments
long technical documents: it breaks multi-page tables, separates equations from their defini-

1.2.8
State-of-the-art RAG Architecture for Scientific Literature
To address these limitations, several purpose-built scientific RAG systems have emerged.
PaperQA (Lála et al., 2023) [29] introduced an agent that performs full-text retrieval across
scientific articles with an explicit relevance-assessment step before generation, matching expert
human researchers on the LitQA biomedical benchmark (69.5 % vs. 66.8 %). Its successor
PaperQA2 (Skarlinski et al., 2024) [48] extends this with a three-phase agentic workflow —
Paper Search, Gather Evidence, and Generate Answer — combined with document-metadata-
aware embeddings, an LLM-based reranking and contextual-summarization (RCS) module,
and a citation-graph navigation tool. PaperQA2 achieves state-of-the-art performance on the
science portion of the RAG-QA Arena benchmark, scoring 12.4 % higher than the closest
competing system across 1,404 questions and 1.7 million documents. More recent work pushes
the architecture further: Nagori et al. (2025) [37] couple a Llama-3.3-70B router agent over a
Neo4j citation knowledge graph with a hybrid FAISS + BM25 vector store, while RAG-Anything
(Han et al., 2025) [18] targets the multimodal bottleneck through graph-aware indexing of figures

1.3
Literature Gap
The literature reviewed in §1.2 indicates that each of the three predictive routes for transition
— empirical correlation, transition-sensitive Reynolds-averaged modeling, and hot-wire exper-
imentation — is individually mature, with well-developed theoretical foundations, accepted
benchmark cases, and routine use in engineering practice. What is absent is a systematic mech-
anism by which these three routes can inform one another within a single, auditable workflow.
Cross-validation between theoretical predictions, numerical results, and experimental measure-
ments is widely accepted as desirable, but is, in practice, conducted by hand for individual
flow cases, with information passing between the three streams through written reports and the
personal judgment of the investigator. The cross-validation step that closes a transition study
is consequently seldom documented in a form that another investigator can reproduce, and the
diagnostic information that experimental observations could feed back to the predictive routes
is rarely incorporated in an explicit manner.
A second gap lies in the application of modern agentic frameworks to this class of problem.
The architectures surveyed in §1.2.5 — orchestrator–agent decomposition, retrieval-augmented
reasoning, and deterministic tool invocation — have been demonstrated in chemistry, molecular
biology, materials science, and observational astronomy. To the author’s knowledge, however,
no published work has so far applied such a framework either to boundary-layer transition
or, more broadly, to any topic in fluid mechanics. A further methodological consideration is
that the safeguards required to prevent unsupported or fabricated content in language-model
output, including source tracking of reported quantities, deterministic verification and dedicated
critique stages, are typically deployed in an advisory capacity rather than as enforced constraints
on the workflow itself. The reliable use of language-model agents in scientific work therefore
remains an open problem in the broader agentic-science literature.
The present thesis is positioned in these gaps. The framework developed in the chapters that
follow is intended to integrate the empirical, numerical, and experimental analyses of transition
within a single agentic workflow whose intermediate quantities, decisions, and outputs are

1.4
Objectives
In view of the literature gaps identified in §1.3, the present thesis pursues the following
objectives. The architecture realising these objectives is described in Chapter 3.
Framework design. To develop a multi-agent framework that integrates empirical, numer-
ical and experimental analyses of boundary-layer transition within a single, auditable workflow
coordinated by a deterministic orchestration layer; the orchestration layer is required to sup-

and retrospective feedback, in which experimental measurements are returned to the predictive
branches for diagnostic re-examination of their assumptions.
Deterministic safeguards. To incorporate within this framework a set of safeguards —

To demonstrate the framework on a representative zero-

Chapter 2
Methodology
This chapter outlines the framework developed to investigate transitional boundary-layer
flows. The study is organized around four main components: prediction of transition onset and
extent through empirical correlations; transition-sensitive Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes
(RANS) simulation of the same flow; experimental identification of transition characteristics
from hot-wire measurements; and detailed boundary-layer characterization from the same
experimental data. In addition, the study incorporates retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
and an agentic AI framework to integrate these traditionally independent approaches into a
unified research workflow, thereby enabling automated prediction, experimental planning, data
analysis, and validation.
2.1
Empirical correlations used for prediction of transition
onset and extent
Boundary-layer transition correlations are developed by fitting algebraic relations to tran-
sition data obtained from controlled experimental investigations and, in some cases, from
high-fidelity simulations, notably Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS). The resulting relations
provide estimates of transition characteristics — the onset location, the transition extent, or the
transition Reynolds number — from a limited set of flow parameters. The predictive capability
of an empirical correlation is inherently linked to the dataset from which it was derived: the
underlying experiments or simulations are conducted under specific flow conditions, includ-
ing free-stream turbulence intensity, pressure-gradient environment, Reynolds-number range,
surface characteristics, and the method of disturbance generation. For instance, passive grids,
active grids, upstream roughness elements, and trip devices produce turbulence fields with
different integral length scales, decay characteristics, and levels of isotropy.
Several correlation families are widely used for boundary-layer transition on a flat plate
under free-stream turbulence. Abu-Ghannam & Shaw [1] is derived from flat-plate experiments
under zero, adverse, and favorable pressure gradients, and is recommended for Tu = 0.3–5%.
Mayle [35] is based on a broader review of flat-plate and turbomachinery transition data and
applies to Tu = 1–10%. Fransson & Shahinfar [16] present an experimental campaign in which
both Tu = 1.81–6.19% and Λ_x = 16–26 mm are varied and derive a semi-empirical model that
includes both Tu and Λ_x in the transition prediction. Gonzalez, Agrawal & Wu [17] derive an

2.2
Computational Methods for Prediction of Transition On-
set and Extent
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) provides a physics-based approach for predicting
boundary-layer transition through the numerical solution of the governing flow equations.
Among the available CFD methodologies, Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models
offer a practical balance between computational cost and predictive capability. In this study,
transition prediction is performed using transition-sensitive RANS models implemented within
the OpenFOAM framework.
2.2.1
Transition Modeling in RANS
The conventional RANS approach decomposes each instantaneous flow quantity into a
time-averaged mean and a fluctuating component and applies time-averaging to the Navier–
Stokes equations. Turbulence models, such as the k–𝜔family, are formulated primarily for fully
turbulent flows and therefore do not explicitly account for the laminar-to-turbulent transition
process and cannot, by themselves, reliably predict the onset and extent of transition.
To overcome this limitation, transition-sensitive RANS models introduce additional trans-
port equations for variables that represent key transition mechanisms, such as laminar kinetic
energy, intermittency, or a transition-onset Reynolds number. These variables evolve based on
local flow conditions and interact with the turbulence model to regulate turbulence production
within the boundary layer. The converged solution provides distributions of quantities such as
skin friction and intermittency, from which the onset and extent of transition can be identified
by analyzing their spatial variation.
2.2.2
k–k_L–𝜔Transition Model
The Walters & Cokljat [53] model treats transition as the amplification of disturbances
within a laminar boundary layer. These disturbances may originate from upstream turbulence,
surface imperfections, vibrations, or acoustic noise.
The growth of these disturbances is
represented through a transport equation for laminar kinetic energy, which serves as a measure

16
of disturbance intensity. Within this formulation, production terms represent the amplification
of pre-transitional fluctuations, while dissipation terms account for their decay in stable regions.
As the balance shifts in favor of production, the laminar boundary layer becomes unstable
and progressively transitions to turbulence.
The model is coupled with a k–𝜔turbulence
framework, where the evolving laminar kinetic energy field modulates turbulence production,
thereby regulating the onset and spatial extent of transition.
2.2.3
𝛾–Re_𝜃Transitional Model
The Langtry–Menter [30] 𝛾–Re_𝜃model treats transition as the gradual emergence and
growth of turbulent regions within a laminar boundary layer. It is a correlation-based transition
model that extends the k–𝜔SST turbulence framework through the introduction of two additional
transport equations: one for intermittency (𝛾), which represents the local level of turbulent
activity, and another for the transition momentum-thickness Reynolds number Re_𝜃t, which
provides an empirical criterion for transition onset based on free-stream turbulence intensity
and pressure-gradient effects.
The resulting four-equation framework, comprising the SST transport equations for k and 𝜔
together with transport equations for 𝛾and Re_𝜃t, incorporates transition physics through the
interaction of these additional variables with the underlying SST model. The Re_𝜃t equation
determines when local flow conditions become favorable for transition, while the intermittency
equation governs the spatial development of the transition region. As intermittency increases, it
progressively activates the turbulence production term in the coupled k–𝜔SST model, allowing
a smooth evolution from laminar to fully turbulent flow. By relying entirely on local flow
quantities, the model avoids non-local boundary-layer integrations and can predict bypass tran-
sition, separation-induced transition, and free-stream-turbulence-induced transition in complex
geometries.
2.3
Experimental Methodology
The experimental part of the study was designed to obtain time-resolved velocity measure-
ments across the boundary-layer transition region. The measurements were carried out using
hot-wire anemometry in a controlled free-stream turbulence environment. The experimental
arrangement comprised an open-circuit wind tunnel, a passive turbulence-generating grid, a
flat-plate test model, a hot-wire anemometry system, and a traversing mechanism for probe
positioning.

2.4
Transition Detection and Characterization
2.4.1
Intermittency-Based Transition Detection
The principal quantity used to characterize transition is the intermittency, 𝛾, defined as the
fraction of time for which the flow is locally turbulent at a given measurement location. In
the present work, the hot-wire velocity signals were calibrated to velocity using King’s law
(Bruun [5]), and intermittency was then evaluated using the procedure proposed by Fransson,
Matsubara and Alfredsson [15]. The method employs a high-pass-filtered velocity signal to
construct a detector function, from which a criterion function is obtained by temporal averaging.

Intermittency is subsequently determined through a threshold-sweep procedure, providing an
estimate that is less sensitive to the choice of a single threshold than direct counting methods.
2.4.2
Validation of the Intermittency Estimate
As an additional consistency check, a conditional-averaging procedure based on the squared
streamwise velocity gradient was applied to the threshold-sweep intermittency estimate at
each measurement station. By comparing the level of fluctuation activity inside and outside
the detected spot intervals, the procedure distinguishes stations at which the estimate reflects
genuine spot detection from those at which it is dominated by background fluctuations. The
detailed formulation is described in Chapter 3.

2.6
Multi-Agent LLM Architecture
The AI framework is organized as a collection of specialized agents, each responsible for
a well-defined stage of the predict–experiment–analyze workflow. Information is exchanged
between agents through structured intermediate states under the control of a deterministic
orchestration layer, enabling modular execution, transparent data flow, and localized error
handling. The detailed operation of each agent within this framework is described in Chapter 3.

2.6.1
Retrieval-Augmented Generation
The framework employs retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to ground language-model
reasoning in domain-specific literature. A curated collection of research papers on boundary-
layer transition is indexed in a searchable vector database, from which relevant information is
retrieved before an agent formulates its response. Throughout the present work, this mechanism
is used to support literature review, identify appropriate empirical correlations and computa-
tional models, and ensure that methodological decisions and reported equations are grounded
in published sources rather than relying solely on the internal knowledge of the language model.
The detailed architecture of Agent 1, including its RAG implementation for retrieval of relevant
content from a research-paper corpus, is presented in Appendix B.
2.6.2
Tool Calling and Structured Computation
Within the proposed framework, the language model is responsible for reasoning and work-
flow orchestration, while numerical computations are delegated to deterministic software tools.
Operations such as evaluating empirical correlations, processing hot-wire measurements, fitting
calibration curves, and computing boundary-layer parameters are performed through dedicated
computational routines. This separation of reasoning from numerical execution improves re-
producibility, ensures unit consistency, and eliminates arithmetic errors associated with direct
language-model computation.
2.6.3
Verification and Auditability
To ensure reliability and scientific traceability, the outputs generated by the framework are
subjected to structured validation before being propagated through the workflow. Retrieved liter-
ature, generated equations, and computational results are verified against predefined constraints
and source documents, while the execution history of individual agents, tool invocations, and
intermediate outputs is retained as an audit trail. This provides transparency in the reasoning
process and enables the complete analysis to be reproduced and independently verified.

Chapter 3
Workflow
This chapter describes the architecture of the agentic workflow developed to investigate the
characteristics of transitional boundary-layer flow. Each agent within the framework focuses on
a distinct aspect of the transition problem, so that the corresponding physics can be addressed
with the depth appropriate to that stage.
Attention is given to the safeguards that prevent
the language-model components from generating unsupported or ’hallucinated’ content. The
following sections present the overall architecture and describe the role of each agent.
3.1
Overview of the Proposed Agentic Framework
The workflow is coordinated by an orchestrator layer that manages information flow between
the individual agents. Rather than interacting directly with each agent, the user provides a
single natural-language description of the transition problem through a unified interface. The
orchestrator is responsible for interpreting this request, determining which agents must be
activated, and supplying each agent with a task-specific representation of the problem.
The first responsibility of the orchestrator is query decomposition. A single user request
often contains information relevant to multiple branches of the framework, including theoretical
prediction, CFD simulation, and experimental planning. Instead of forwarding the original
query unchanged, the orchestrator generates agent-specific queries tailored to the requirements
of each branch.
For example, the theoretical agent receives a query focused on empirical
transition prediction, whereas the CFD agent receives a version emphasizing simulation setup
and modeling requirements. Similarly, the experimental agent receives a query centred on
measurement planning and expected transition behavior.
After query tailoring, the orchestrator initiates the workflow. The theoretical (A1) and CFD
(A2) branches are executed independently and in parallel. Their outputs are collected on com-
pletion and merged into a transition prediction envelope that represents the expected transition
region according to the available predictive methods. This envelope is then supplied to the
experimental branch, where it guides pilot-experiment planning and subsequent measurement
activities.
The orchestrator also manages information exchange between agents. Outputs produced by
one agent are converted into structured hand-off objects that can be consumed by downstream
agents without requiring further user intervention. The theoretical predictions from A1 and the
CFD predictions from A2 are supplied to A3 to guide pilot-experiment planning. The transition

3.1.1
Agent 1: Theoretical Prediction Workflow
Agent 1 (A1) constitutes the theoretical branch of the framework and is responsible for
generating an initial estimate of the transition behavior from the user-specified flow conditions.
The agent is invoked immediately after the orchestrator completes query decomposition and
supplies a theory-oriented representation of the problem.
The workflow begins with the extraction of structured flow parameters from the tailored
query. These parameters may include the freestream velocity, turbulence intensity, viscosity,
geometry description, pressure-gradient condition, roughness information, turbulence length
scale, and turbulence-generation characteristics. The extracted information is stored as a struc-
tured flow-state representation that serves as the input for all subsequent calculations. In addition
to the values extracted from natural-language input, the agent accepts a complementary set of
facility-specific overrides supplied directly by the operator. When available, quantities such
as freestream velocity, turbulence intensity, viscosity, chord length, integral length scale, and
boundary-layer origin are merged into the flow state before downstream reasoning begins. This
ensures that experimentally calibrated quantities take precedence over values inferred from the
query text.
The structured flow state is then passed to the literature-intelligence layer, which retrieves
transition correlations, supporting boundary-layer relations, and relevant methodological infor-
mation from the indexed literature corpus. The purpose of this stage is to identify candidate
prediction models that are applicable to the specified flow regime.
Retrieval is performed
adaptively rather than as a single pass. Following each retrieval cycle, the recovered evidence
is assessed for relevance and coverage. When gaps are identified, supplementary queries are
generated and the retrieval process is repeated. If no relevant evidence can be identified after the
permitted retrieval iterations, the workflow terminates without producing transition predictions,
thereby preventing unsupported conclusions from being generated from unrelated literature.
The retrieved evidence is supplied to a planning module that determines the sequence
of calculations required to answer the query. A tool-assisted reasoning stage subsequently
evaluates the required equations, computes the requested quantities, and assembles the resulting
transition predictions. The reasoning module may invoke numerical computation, equation
lookup, glossary lookup, and literature-search tools as required during execution.
Once a candidate solution has been produced, the framework performs a deterministic
comparison between the planned and executed calculations.
If essential quantities remain
uncomputed and the workflow budget permits, a revision cycle is initiated in which the reasoning
stage is re-executed with explicit instructions regarding the missing quantities. The revised
output is subsequently evaluated by an independent critique stage that assesses correctness,
completeness, and evidential support before acceptance.

Agent 2: CFD Prediction Workflow
Agent 2 (A2) constitutes the numerical branch of the framework and is responsible for
generating transition predictions through transition-sensitive Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes
(RANS) simulations. The agent is invoked in parallel with A1 following query decomposition
by the orchestrator. The structured flow conditions are supplied at the time of invocation; the
theoretical prediction envelope generated by A1 is incorporated on completion of both branches
and is used for the theory–CFD comparison reported in the A2 output. The full Phase-1 and
Phase-2 workflow of A2 is summarized in Figure 3.3.
Figure 3.3: Workflow of Agent 2.
The workflow begins with a planning stage that determines an appropriate CFD strategy for
the specified flow case. The flow conditions and user constraints are used to construct a CFD
case configuration containing the computational domain, mesh strategy, boundary conditions,
turbulence-model settings, and solver controls. The objective of this stage is to transform a
high-level transition problem into a physically consistent simulation setup suitable for numerical
execution.
Once the initial configuration has been generated, the workflow performs a series of deter-
ministic validation checks. These checks assess the completeness of the case definition, verify
that prescribed quantities remain within physically admissible limits, and ensure consistency
between the selected turbulence model, boundary conditions, and operating conditions. Missing
or inconsistent information is resolved before simulation proceeds.
The validated configuration is subsequently converted into an executable OpenFOAM case.
Mesh-quality assessment is performed prior to solver execution to identify numerical issues
that may compromise solution accuracy or convergence. Following successful validation, the
transition-sensitive RANS simulation is executed and the resulting flow field is processed to
extract transition-relevant quantities.

The primary post-processing stage evaluates quantities such as the skin-friction distribution
and associated transition indicators. These quantities are used to estimate the transition onset
location, transition development, and other flow characteristics required for comparison with

package.
To improve robustness, the workflow incorporates bounded recovery mechanisms. When
solver execution fails or convergence issues are detected, diagnostic routines attempt to iden-
tify the source of the failure and generate corrective actions within predefined limits. This

predictions and experimental observations, including inlet turbulence specification, turbulence-
model behavior, mesh resolution, and boundary-condition assumptions. The purpose of this
stage is not to modify the experimental measurements, but to identify plausible causes of
numerical–experimental discrepancy and guide subsequent investigation.

differences exist between the prediction routes.

3.2
Information Flow and Feedback Mechanisms
The individual agents described in the preceding sections do not operate as independent
analysis modules. Instead, they form a connected workflow in which the output of one stage
becomes the input of subsequent stages. The orchestrator manages these interactions through
structured hand-off objects that permit information to be transferred between agents without
requiring repeated user intervention.
The workflow begins with parallel execution of the theoretical and numerical branches.
Agent 1 produces a theory-based transition prediction envelope, while Agent 2 generates an
independent CFD-based prediction.
These outputs are subsequently merged and supplied
to Agent 3, which uses them to construct a pilot measurement strategy. Consequently, the

Chapter 3. Workflow
36
experimental campaign is informed by both theoretical expectations and numerical predictions
before any measurements are acquired.
The first major feedback pathway is established following completion of the pilot experiment.
Agent 3 generates an intermittency-based estimate of transition onset, transition completion,
turbulence decay, and turbulence length-scale characteristics. These experimentally derived
quantities are returned independently to Agents 1 and 2 through retrospective-analysis pathways
designated A1 Phase 2 and A2 Phase 2. Rather than modifying the experimental observations,
the objective of each feedback loop is to investigate possible causes of disagreement between
prediction and measurement. Theoretical assumptions, turbulence-decay estimates, correla-
tion applicability, inlet turbulence specifications, and CFD modeling choices can therefore be
examined using experimentally derived evidence.
A second information pathway connects the pilot experiment to the full measurement cam-
paign. The experimentally informed transition envelope produced by Agent 3 is supplied to
Agent 4, which uses it to design a transition-informed traverse plan. In this manner, information
obtained from a limited pilot dataset directly influences the allocation of measurement effort
during the full experiment.
Following completion of the full measurement campaign, Agent 5 produces the final exper-
imental dataset consisting of calibrated velocity measurements, statistical summaries, spectral
information, boundary-layer profiles, conditional-averaging diagnostics, and transition indi-
cators. These outputs are subsequently transmitted to Agent 6 together with the predictions
generated by Agents 1 and 2.
The final information-convergence stage occurs within Agent 6. Here, the independently
generated theoretical, CFD, and experimental results are consolidated into a common compar-
ison framework. Agreement metrics are evaluated, discrepancies are classified, and the final
transition assessment is generated. The workflow therefore forms a closed information loop
in which prediction guides measurement, measurement informs diagnosis, and all branches
ultimately converge within a unified cross-validation stage.
The resulting architecture differs from a conventional sequential workflow because experi-
mental evidence is not treated solely as a final validation step. Instead, information generated
during experimentation actively influences both prediction and measurement planning through-
out the workflow.

Summary
This chapter has presented the architecture and execution workflow of the six-agent frame-
work developed in the present work. The framework combines theoretical prediction, com-
putational simulation, and experimental measurement within a coordinated process in which

specialized agents perform distinct tasks while exchanging information through a central or-
chestration layer. The roles of the individual agents, the flow of information between them, and
the feedback pathways linking the theoretical, numerical, and experimental branches have been
described in detail.
Particular emphasis has been placed on the progressive refinement of transition predictions
through the workflow. Initial estimates obtained from empirical correlations and transition-
sensitive CFD simulations are used to guide the experimental campaign, while measurements
obtained during the pilot and full-scale investigations are subsequently returned to the prediction
branches through dedicated retrospective-analysis stages. The framework therefore treats theory,
simulation, and experiment not as independent activities, but as interconnected components of
a single transition-analysis process.
The chapter has also described the mechanisms used to support consistency and traceability
throughout the workflow, including verification procedures, source tracking of reported values,
and structured information exchange between agents. Together, these features are intended to
provide an auditable basis on which the predictions obtained from the different analysis routes
can be compared.

theoretical, numerical, and experimental approaches is assessed, and on which the framework

5.1
Conclusions
The objective of the present study was to develop an agentic AI framework for automating
a research workflow, with a human in the loop, for problems that involve both simulations
and laboratory experiments. The framework was organized into six specialized agents coor-
dinated by an orchestrator, and was demonstrated on the problem of boundary-layer transition
induced by free-stream turbulence over a smooth flat plate at zero pressure gradient. The specific
aim was to estimate the transition onset and end locations 𝑥𝑡and 𝑥𝑒and to perform statistical
analysis on the streamwise-velocity signals obtained from hot-wire anemometry.
The six agents acted in sequence on the same flow case as follows.
Agent 1 retrieved the relevant empirical correlations from the curated transition-literature
corpus and computed the theoretical prediction envelope 𝑥𝑡≈0.32-0.50 m and 𝑥𝑒≈0.88 m,
with the Abu-Ghannam and Shaw (1980) [1] correlation providing the primary onset estimate
𝑥𝑡≈0.32 m.
Agent 2 translated the structured flow specification into transition-sensitive RANS simula-
tions using the 𝛾-f
𝑅𝑒𝜃𝑡and 𝑘-𝑘𝐿-𝜔closures, and reported a CFD onset of 𝑥𝑡≈0.42 m from the
𝐶𝑓-departure criterion.
Agent 3 combined the theory and CFD envelopes, generated the NI-DAQ acquisition script

• Autonomous experimental execution: Connect the agentic workflow directly to the

traverse motion, hot-wire voltage range and online data quality.
• Extension to other transition mechanisms and geometries: Apply the same six-agent

roughness-induced transition, airfoil flows and turbomachinery-relevant geometries to

A.2.3
Threshold sweep and the c-intercept method
For a chosen threshold u_s, an indicator is constructed by simple comparison,
I(t; u_s) = 1 if C(t) > u_s, else 0, (A.17)
and the corresponding intermittency is the temporal fraction
𝛾_obs(u_s) = (1/N) Σ𝑖=1𝑁I(t𝑖; u_s). (A.18)
Equation (A.18) makes explicit that 𝛾_obs depends on the chosen threshold. A single,
manually selected threshold cannot return a defensible estimate of the true intermittency at the
station; the value at low u_s saturates near unity, while at high u_s it falls to a noise floor.
To overcome this ambiguity, Fransson et al. (2005) [15] introduced the c-intercept method.
The threshold u_s is swept over a range that brackets the decay of 𝛾_obs from saturation to
noise floor. On a semilogarithmic axis the decay band is found, to good approximation, to be
well-described by a straight line, i.e.,
𝛾_obs(u_s) ≈c · exp(𝛼· u_s), 𝛼< 0, (A.19)
with c and 𝛼the fit parameters. In the present work the fit is performed on the deep-
decay band 0.005 ≤𝛾_obs ≤0.5 — the same region marked by the dotted vertical lines in Fig
A.1(b) of the original paper — and within those outer limits the actual fit interval is selected
automatically as the longest contiguous run that remains well-described by a straight line on the
semilogarithmic plot. The intercept
𝛾_true = c (A.20)
is taken as the true intermittency at the station. The operating threshold u_s is then read off
the observed sweep curve at the value at which 𝛾_obs(u_s) = c, and the final indicator function
used in all downstream analyses is I(t) ≡I(t; u_s*).
Two points are worth recording. First, the intercept c is extrapolated from the well-behaved
exponential portion of the sweep curve and is therefore insensitive to the precise placement of
the upper saturation and the lower noise-floor regions. Second, the method admits a definite
physical interpretation: c corresponds to the limit of vanishing threshold and may therefore
be associated with the intermittency that would be returned by an ideal detector whose noise
contribution is identically zero. In their original paper, Fransson et al. (2005) reject the two-
line piecewise fit of Kuan and Wang (1990) precisely because the latter does not admit such an
interpretation.

B.1
Introduction
Agent 1 forms the theoretical-prediction component of the proposed multi-agent framework.
Its purpose is to convert a natural-language description of a boundary-layer transition problem
into a source-grounded numerical evaluation of algebraic transition correlations. The agent
receives a user query containing flow conditions, retrieves relevant literature from a curated
transition corpus, identifies applicable empirical relations, evaluates the selected equations using
explicit computation, verifies that the numerical results can be traced to executed computation,
and generates a structured technical report for downstream use.
The motivation for developing Agent 1 arises from a practical difficulty in transition pre-
diction. Empirical correlations for transition onset and transition length are distributed across
several papers and often use different definitions, symbols, calibration ranges, and turbulence-
intensity conventions. A direct language-model response may therefore appear plausible while
silently using an incorrect exponent, Reynolds-number definition, or unit convention. Such
mistakes are especially serious in transition prediction because small algebraic differences can
produce large shifts in predicted onset location.
Agent 1 was therefore designed as a controlled retrieval-and-reasoning workflow rather
than as a single prompt-response system.
Literature retrieval supplies the evidence base.
The planning stage determines which quantities must be evaluated. Numerical calculations
are executed through computational tools.
Formula verification checks whether proposed
expressions are linked to source equations. A subsequent source-verification step then checks
whether the numerical values reported in the final answer can be traced to executed computations.
The agent is intentionally limited to algebraic and closed-form transition-prediction tasks.
It does not solve CFD cases, perform stability analysis, execute DNS or LES, or process
experimental hot-wire signals. Those tasks are handled by other components of the complete

framework. This restricted scope is deliberate: Agent 1 is intended to automate the literature-
to-calculation part of the workflow while preserving traceability and auditability.
The contribution documented in this appendix is not the invention of retrieval-augmented

These are established techniques. The contribution lies in their integration into a domain-

B.2
Design Objectives
The implementation of Agent 1 is guided by five design objectives.
B.2.1
Preservation of the user’s flow case
The natural-language query is treated as the original specification of the problem. Although
the query is parsed into a structured record containing quantities such as freestream velocity,
turbulence intensity, viscosity, geometry, pressure-gradient description, and grid information,
the original query is retained throughout the workflow. This prevents the system from losing
qualitative details that may influence the choice of correlation or interpretation of the result.
B.2.2
Literature-grounded correlation selection
Agent 1 does not rely only on the language model’s internal knowledge of transition corre-
lations. Before planning or computation, the agent retrieves relevant passages from an indexed
corpus of boundary-layer transition literature. This retrieval step is essential because transition
correlations are valid only within particular calibration regimes. The selected equation must
therefore be supported by source material rather than by model memory alone.
B.2.3
Separation of reasoning and numerical computation
The language model is used to interpret the query, decide which sources are relevant, and
organize the calculation. However, numerical values are produced through explicit computation.
This design prevents the result from being based on unsupported mental arithmetic generated
by the model. A reported numerical value is considered trustworthy only when it can be traced
to an executed computation or is explicitly marked as qualitative.

B.2.4
Formula-level verification
A formula-verification layer is included before numerical execution. This layer checks gen-
erated computation code for tracked variables, equation tags, and hard-coded result assignments.
Where possible, the proposed expression is compared with the corresponding source-equation
entry. This reduces the risk that the model cites one equation but implements another, or that it
substitutes a final numerical value in place of an actual algebraic computation.
B.2.5
Auditable final output
The output of Agent 1 is intended to be auditable. The final report is accompanied by
structured intermediate information, including retrieved evidence, planning output, tool calls,
critique information, value-source verification status, and generated diagnostic artefacts. If a
numerical value cannot be traced to computation, it is flagged rather than silently accepted.
This makes the result suitable for thesis use, where the reasoning path must be defensible.
B.3
Overall Architecture
Agent 1 is implemented as a staged retrieval-and-reasoning pipeline. The workflow begins
with query parsing and proceeds through adaptive retrieval, planning, tool-assisted reason-
ing, coverage checking, critique, value reconciliation, source verification of reported values,
diagnostic plotting, report generation, and persistence of run artefacts.
The ordering of the stages is intentional. Retrieval precedes planning so that the computation
plan is based on the literature corpus. Planning precedes reasoning so that the reasoner operates
from a defined list of quantities, source papers, and dependencies. Formula verification is
attached to computation so that proposed equations can be rejected before they influence the
result. Source verification follows reasoning so that the values appearing in the final report can
be checked against the actual computed values.
The implemented system can be viewed as three interacting layers. The first is the or-
chestration layer, which controls the execution sequence and state transitions. The second is
the retrieval layer, which constructs the evidence pool from the literature corpus using both
semantic and lexical search. The third is the reasoning-and-verification layer, which performs
tool-assisted numerical evaluation and checks whether the final reported values are traceable.

A single retrieval strategy is generally insufficient for this task. Dense semantic retrieval can

phrase, the system retrieves evidence from multiple perspectives, including transition onset,
transition length, turbulence effects, governing equations, and applicability constraints.
Candidate passages returned by the retrieval engines are merged into a common evidence
pool and subsequently reranked using a cross-encoder model. Unlike vector similarity measures,
which evaluate the query and passage independently before comparison, the cross-encoder
jointly evaluates both texts and produces a relevance score based on their combined context. This
reranking stage substantially improves the ordering of retrieved evidence and helps prioritize
equation-bearing passages over loosely related explanatory text.
The retrieval stage is adaptive rather than single-pass. After reranking, the retrieved evidence
is evaluated for sufficiency. If important information is missing — for example, if a correlation
is identified but its governing equation cannot be found — the system generates supplementary
retrieval queries and performs an additional retrieval cycle. This iterative process continues
until the evidence pool is considered sufficient or a predefined retrieval budget is exhausted.
The objective of adaptive retrieval is not to maximize the number of retrieved documents but
to maximize the completeness of the evidence available to the planning and reasoning stages.
The final output of retrieval is therefore a validated evidence pool containing the passages judged
most relevant to the engineering problem under consideration.

The purpose of planning is to convert retrieved evidence into a structured computation
plan. Rather than allowing the reasoning engine to determine the entire calculation pathway
dynamically, the planner first identifies the quantities required to answer the query, the equations
that should be evaluated, and the dependencies between intermediate and final variables. This
decomposition reduces the likelihood of omitted calculations and provides a structured target
against which later coverage verification can be performed.
The planning process begins by analyzing the user query together with the validated evidence

pool. Relevant correlations, governing equations, and supporting relations are identified from
the retrieved literature. The planner then determines which quantities are directly available
from the user input and which quantities must be derived through intermediate calculations.
For example, evaluation of a transition-onset correlation may require local turbulence intensity,
momentum-thickness Reynolds number, or boundary-layer quantities that are not explicitly
provided in the original query.
The resulting computation plan is represented as an ordered sequence of tasks. Each task
contains the quantity to be evaluated, the source equation or reference from which it originates,
the required inputs, and any dependencies on preceding tasks. The plan therefore serves as a
bridge between literature retrieval and numerical execution.
A key advantage of explicit planning is that it separates problem decomposition from
computation. The reasoning engine is not required to infer the entire solution strategy during
execution because the major computational steps have already been identified. This improves
transparency and enables later workflow stages to verify whether all planned quantities have
been successfully evaluated.
The final output of the planning stage is a structured computation plan that guides the
tool-assisted reasoning engine and provides the reference framework used by the coverage-
verification stage.

Rather than generating a complete answer directly from retrieved text, Agent 1 follows a tool-
assisted reasoning paradigm. The language model is responsible for interpreting the evidence,
selecting appropriate equations, determining the sequence of calculations, and deciding when
additional information is required. Numerical computation itself is delegated to specialized
tools.
This design was adopted because empirical transition correlations frequently involve mul-
tiple intermediate quantities and non-trivial dependencies. For example, the evaluation of a
transition-onset correlation may require freestream turbulence intensity, momentum-thickness
Reynolds number, turbulence-decay relations, or auxiliary boundary-layer quantities. Perform-

ing such calculations entirely through language-model reasoning increases the risk of transcrip-
tion errors, incorrect substitutions, and unsupported arithmetic. Tool-assisted execution reduces
these risks by separating symbolic reasoning from numerical evaluation.
The reasoning process follows a ReAct-style workflow. At each step, the model analyzes
the current state of the problem, determines whether additional information is required, invokes
an appropriate tool if necessary, observes the returned result, and updates its reasoning state
before proceeding to the next step. This cycle continues until all quantities required by the
computation plan have been evaluated or the termination criteria are reached.
The primary tools available to the reasoner are computation, equation lookup, glossary
lookup, and targeted retrieval. The compute tool executes numerical calculations in a controlled
Python environment and returns structured outputs. The equation lookup tool retrieves stored
source equations from the equation inventory. The glossary lookup tool provides canonical
definitions and notation mappings for technical quantities.
The retrieval tool enables the
reasoner to obtain additional evidence when the current evidence pool is insufficient.
Unlike a conventional conversational agent, Agent 1 does not treat tool outputs as optional
suggestions. Tool outputs become part of the working state and are subsequently available to the
verification, critique, and source-checking stages. This ensures that numerical results produced
during execution remain traceable throughout the remainder of the workflow.
B.8

exponent, use a different Reynolds-number definition, apply the wrong turbulence-intensity
convention, or directly report a plausible numerical value without executing the corresponding
calculation.

B.8.1
Motivation
Transition correlations are particularly susceptible to transcription and implementation er-
rors because many relations differ only through small algebraic modifications. For example,
two correlations may use the same variables but employ different exponents, empirical con-
stants, or turbulence-intensity conventions. Such differences may appear minor when expressed
symbolically but can produce substantial changes in the predicted onset location.
Conventional retrieval-augmented workflows generally assume that once a relevant passage
has been retrieved, the language model will correctly interpret and implement the associated
equation. Agent 1 does not make this assumption. Instead, proposed computations are subjected
to explicit verification before numerical execution.
B.8.2
Equation inventory
The verification process is enabled by a structured equation inventory constructed during
corpus preparation. For selected papers, governing equations are stored together with asso-
ciated metadata, including paper identifier, equation identifier, page reference, and symbolic
representation. This inventory serves as a reference against which generated computational
expressions can be compared.
The equation inventory is not intended to replace literature retrieval. Rather, it provides
a structured representation of key equations that allows the verification stage to reason about
mathematical equivalence and detect implementation errors.
B.8.3
Verification of proposed computations
Whenever the reasoning engine proposes a computation, the corresponding code is ana-
lyzed before execution. The verification stage first identifies assignments to tracked variables
associated with engineering quantities of interest. These include quantities such as transition
Reynolds numbers, onset locations, transition lengths, intermittency-related parameters, and
other outputs that may appear in the final report.
The proposed computation is then inspected for hard-coded assignments. A direct assign-
ment of a numerical result to a tracked quantity is treated as suspicious because it bypasses
the computational pathway intended to produce that quantity. Such assignments are therefore
rejected unless explicitly justified by the workflow.
If the computation references a source equation, the verification stage retrieves the corre-
sponding equation from the equation inventory and compares it with the generated expression.
This comparison is designed to detect differences in empirical constants, exponents, variable
definitions, and mathematical structure. Computational expressions that are inconsistent with

the stored equation are rejected and returned to the reasoning stage for revision.
The objective of verification is not to prove mathematical correctness in a formal sense,
but to ensure that the generated computation remains consistent with the source equation upon
which it claims to be based.
B.8.4
Source checking of reported values
Formula verification reduces the likelihood of incorrect computations entering the workflow,
but it does not guarantee that the final report will use the computed values. A language model
may execute a correct calculation and subsequently restate the result incorrectly during report
generation. Agent 1 therefore performs a second validation step after reasoning is complete.
The source-checking stage collects numerical outputs generated during computation and
compares them with the values reported in the final findings. Quantities that can be traced
directly to executed calculations are marked as verified. Quantities that cannot be associated
with a corresponding computation are flagged as unverified.
Where possible, discrepancies between computed values and reported values are reconciled
automatically. The system therefore treats executed computation as the authoritative source of
numerical information rather than relying solely on generated prose.
This design establishes a clear distinction between calculated quantities and narrative expla-
nation. Numerical values derive from computation, whereas the language model is primarily
responsible for interpretation, organization, and presentation.
B.8.5
Role within the workflow
The verification and source-checking stages occupy a unique position within Agent 1. Re-
trieval establishes what information is available, planning determines what should be computed,
and reasoning determines how the computation should proceed. Verification and source check-
ing, however, determine whether the resulting numerical outputs can be trusted.
By separating these responsibilities, Agent 1 reduces the likelihood that a fluent but unsup-
ported answer will be accepted as a valid engineering result. The final report therefore reflects
not only the conclusions of the reasoning process but also the successful completion of a se-
quence of verification checks designed to maintain traceability between literature, computation,
and reported output.

accepted without review.

execution traces, evidence summaries, diagnostic outputs, and agent-handoff records, ensuring
traceability, reproducibility, and information transfer within the broader framework.

Introduction
Boundary-layer transition is the hinge on which a
large fraction of practical low-speed aerodynamics
turns. The streamwise location xt at which the lami-
nar layer first admits intermittent turbulent spots, the

the wall-heat-flux distribution, the local momentum-
thickness Reynolds number that downstream stability

lifting surfaces, intakes, blades, and surface-mounted

and in essentially every industrial setting in which

that classical natural-transition theory addresses, but

Full-traverse envelope
The pilot (xt, xe) = (611.3, 1324.0) mm consensus is
converted, deterministically, into the dense traverse
plan that the second campaign will execute. The con-
struction relies on three inputs: the pilot transition
window, the rig kinematics of the IISc tunnel traverse
(finite y-stroke, finite x-rail length, operator-defined
safe retract height, firmware interlock on descents
below a configured floor), and a deterministic dis-
cretisation policy — uniform spacing in x across the
transition strip and log-spaced points in y within each
station, chosen so that the inner-layer (y+ ≲30) and
outer-wake region are resolved with roughly the same
number of points per decade of y/δ. No empirical
correlation enters this stage; the trajectory is pure
109

geometry.
The planned campaign comprises Nstn = 198 sta-
tions.
At every station the probe is parked at a
constant safe retract height of ysafe = 50.0 mm above
the plate during streamwise translation, descended
to the planned bottom-up y-sweep, returned to ysafe
at the end of the sweep, and only then translated to
the next x-station. The plate surface is at y = 0 by
convention, so ysafe = 50.0 mm is the full clearance
between sensor wire and plate during x-moves. The

Phase 2 retrospective diagnostics
Once the measured xt = 611.3 mm landed from the
Agent 3 pilot, the orchestrator auto-triggered the
Agent 1 and Agent 2 Phase 2 retrospective diagnos-
tics. Each Phase 2 takes the corresponding Phase 1
prediction, the measured onset, and the saved configu-
ration of Phase 1, and performs an inverse parameter
sweep across the physically meaningful range of each
Phase 1 input to identify the single parameter whose
back-fit best closes the prediction–measurement gap.
The output is a ranked list of root-cause candidates
together with a plausibility tag drawn from the cali-
bration band of the originating correlation or closure.
7.1
Agent 1 Phase 2
The theoretical Phase 1 prediction xt = 314.8 mm
under-predicts the measurement 611.3 mm by 296.5
mm (48.5%). The Phase 2 inverse sweep identifies the
leading-edge integral length scale Λx as the primary
root cause: the fitted back-value is 20.05 mm, against
the Phase 1 assumed value of 12.79 mm derived from
the grid spec through the Roach (1987) far-field power
law. The plausibility tag for this candidate is HIGH,
with supporting evidence from Fransson and Shahin-
far (2020) §3.6 and Matsubara and Alfredsson (2001)
§2.3. The secondary candidate is the leading-edge tur-
bulence intensity TuLE, fitted back to 2.20% against
the nominal 2.70%, also HIGH plausibility; the ter-
tiary candidate is the Fransson decay exponent b,
fitted to 1.02 against the published value 0.6, tagged
LOW plausibility because the back-fit falls outside the
conventional band. The recommended next actions
are to measure Λx directly from a hot-wire autocorre-
lation at the leading edge — a step which, in fact, has
already been carried out in this run with a measured
Λx = 39.4 mm that sits even further from the Phase 1
assumed 12.79 mm than the Phase 2 back-fit suggests
— and to run the Fransson decay sub-flow on a Tu(x)
traverse so that C, x0, and b are fitted for the rig
rather than assumed.
7.2
Agent 2 Phase 2
The CFD planner Phase 1 prediction xt = 418.0 mm
under-predicts the measurement 611.3 mm by 193.3
mm (31.6%). The Phase 2 retrospective ranks the
CFD setup choices in order of how much each could
close the prediction–measurement gap if swapped
within its rulebook-defined alternatives.
The pri-
mary root cause identified is the inlet convention:
the current setup used the Langtry–Menter default
calibration tag gammaReTheta, and Phase 2 recom-
mends swapping to the Walters and Cokljat (2008)
convention tag walters_2008 on the grounds that
TuLE = 2.7% sits closer to the published mid-point of
walters_2008 than to the active default. The plausibil-
ity tag for this swap is MEDIUM. The secondary can-
didate is the turbulence-model choice; for this regime
the rulebook flags kOmegaSSTLM as the published
default for T3A-style cases (which is already the active
model), so the secondary recommendation effectively
reduces to “no change,” with supporting evidence
from Dick and Kubacki (2017) §§2–3. The tertiary
candidate is the inlet method: Phase 2 reports that
the Spalart–Rumsey-aware inlet recipe could not be
evaluated because a measured Λx had not been folded

the recommendation therefore defaults to a literature-

walters_2008}.
The recommendation to swap convention is con-
sistent with the Phase 1 finding that the kkLOmega

ther downstream than the kOmegaSSTLM sub-run
(0.774 m vs 0.418 m on the same flow case); the

8
Conclusions
The methodological claim of this work is that an
autonomous, large-language-model-orchestrated anal-
ysis pipeline can carry a single natural-language state-
ment of a low-speed boundary-layer-transition case
through theory, transition-sensitive CFD, and a fully
calibrated hot-wire experiment, and return an in-
ternally consistent manuscript with every numerical
decision traceable through state files and per-stage
citations to either a peer-reviewed source or an on-
the-fly fit. The pipeline has done so here for a bypass
case at U∞= 6.2 m/s and TuLE = 2.7% on a 1.6
m ZPG flat plate in the LT1 tunnel of the IISc Low-
Speed Aerodynamics Laboratory, with the FLOW
block, the override on ν, the correlation choices, the
LCTM seed, the King’s-law anchors, the calibration
mode, the per-station γ values, the Narasimha fit, the
integral parameters, and the conditional acceleration-
variance peaks all carried in the audit trail of a single
run. The pipeline’s contribution as a method is this
end-to-end traceability, exercised across three inde-
pendent pillars within a single execution.
The scientific claim is that on this case the three
pillars bracket the transition onset, agree on the ge-
ometry of the transition zone to within the calibration
windows of their respective correlations, and diverge
on xt in a manner consistent with the physical mecha-
nism of length-scale dependence in bypass transition.
The headline numbers are: theoretical xt = 341.7
mm with Λx = 14.14 mm and xend = 879 mm; CFD
planner xt = 418.0 mm; measured xt = 611.3 mm,
xγ=0.5 = 866.4 mm, xe = 1324.0 mm and Λx = 39.4
mm. The theoretical pillar’s onset is biased upstream
of the measurement by ∼270 mm; the CFD planner’s
onset is biased upstream by ∼190 mm. Both biases
are consistent with the experimental Λx being substan-
tially larger than the LE length scale assumed by the
bypass-regime correlation library and with the unver-
ified leading-edge Tu assumed by the LCTM closure.
The conditional ⟨(∂u/∂t)2⟩diagnostic adds a wall-
normal structural observable whose peak migrates
from y/δ99 ≈0.75 at x = 410 mm to y/δ99 ≈0.08
at x = 1400 mm — a direct experimental signature
of the reorganisation from a spot-dominated early
turbulent layer to a wall-attached, near-equilibrium
turbulent boundary layer — and exposes residual in-
termittency at the lowest wall-normal positions of the
x = 610 mm station that the median γ alone hides.
Future work splits naturally along the same three
pillars. On the theory side, the FS20-family correla-
tions should be revisited with the measured Λx = 39.4
mm, and the AGS prediction should be flagged as
length-scale-blind whenever it is used in design at this
Tu range. On the CFD side, the workstation-resident
OpenFOAM run with all five pre-flight gates passed —
grid-to-LE Tu decay, µt/µ ratio, y+ estimate, domain-
fit, and f
Reθt-seed consistency — is required before the
CFD pillar can be promoted from a planner estimate
to a converged result. On the experimental side, the
dense traverse should be repeated with a fresh in-run
King’s-law sweep, additional low-RPM calibration
anchors to tighten the deep-in-BL extrapolation, a
denser x-grid in the 610–870 mm intermittent band
so that the conditional acceleration-variance diagnos-
tic operates at its full discriminating power, and a
wall-normal traverse at the FMA-optimal y/δ ≈0.4
so that the absolute γ levels are placement-insensitive.
The conditional ⟨(∂u/∂t)2⟩diagnostic, having shown
substantive new physical content at the single gen-
uinely intermittent station of the present survey, is the
leading candidate observable for that next campaign.
